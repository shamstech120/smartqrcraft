Are QR Codes Safe? How to Spot QR Code Scams

Scanning a QR code is not dangerous by itself. The risk is what the code sends you to, and criminals count on you not checking. Here is how QR scams work and how to stay safe.

The short answer

A QR code is just text stored in a pattern. When your camera scans it, it reads that text and offers an action, usually opening a link. Nothing harmful happens in the scan itself. The danger comes from the next step: opening a fake website, entering a password, approving a payment or installing something.

Security researchers call QR code phishing quishing. Consumer protection and law enforcement agencies, including the FTC in the US and the FBI, have published warnings about it.

How QR code scams work

These are the common patterns.

  • Sticker overlays. A fake code is stuck over a real one on a parking meter, poster, menu or restaurant table, sending payments or logins to a criminal.
  • Unexpected mail or packages. A letter or parcel with a QR code that asks you to confirm a delivery, a payment or an account.
  • Fake urgency in email. An email with a QR code says your account is locked or a payment failed. Because it is an image, some email filters do not read it.
  • Fake support or login pages. The link goes to a page that looks like your bank or a well-known brand and asks for a password or card.
  • Payment swaps. A code is replaced with the scammer's payment details. Someone tells you to scan to receive money, but scanning in a payment app sends money out.
  • Malicious apps. A code leads to a download that is not from the official app store.

A checklist before you scan

  1. Look at the physical code. Is it stuck on? Does the edge of another sticker show?
  2. Preview the content. Use a scanner that shows the link before it opens, such as the SmartQRCraft scanner, and read the domain.
  3. Check the domain. Does it match the company? Watch for extra words, odd endings and misspellings.
  4. Do not enter passwords or card details on a page you reached only through a code. Type the address yourself or use the official app.
  5. Be extra careful with payments. Confirm the payee name and the amount before you approve.
  6. When in doubt, skip it. Go to the company's website yourself.

If you put QR codes in public

Businesses can protect customers too.

  • Check your codes regularly. Look for stickers placed over yours, especially in public places.
  • Print the destination in words near the code, so people can compare.
  • Use tamper-resistant materials where codes are exposed, or place them behind glass or on hard-to-cover surfaces.
  • Send people to your own domain, not a shortened link you cannot verify.
  • Never ask customers for passwords through a code.
  • Test your own code with the QR code tester after you print.

If you think you have been scammed

Stop and do not enter more information. If you entered a password, change it on the real site and on any other account that uses it. If you approved a payment, contact your bank or payment provider immediately, because speed matters. Report the scam to the relevant authority in your country and to the service being impersonated.
